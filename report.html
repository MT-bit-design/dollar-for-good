Dollar for Good

A charity impact tool with sourced numbers, plain math and no guesses · Final project · Michael Thompson

GitHub repo: https://github.com/MT-bit-design/dollar-for-good (to be published before submission)
App: runs locally from a fresh clone in about 5 minutes (see the README). It is not hosted online.
Model: gpt-6-luna via Portkey (course key)

A donor picks a cause and sees charities ranked by how much good each dollar does, in that cause's own unit. A funder uploads the charities they fund and sees where each one sits against a sourced benchmark.

2. Motivation

Who has this problem. Two kinds of people want to know "how much good does a dollar do here?":

  • Individual donors who give a few hundred to a few thousand dollars a year. They pick charities by name recognition, a friend's ask, or overhead ratios. The overhead ratio is the one number most charity websites put up front, and it says nothing about impact.
  • Small foundations and family offices, plus the program officers who run them. They fund 10–50 charities across several causes, and their board asks every year whether the money is doing as much good as it could. They answer by reading annual reports one at a time.

How they deal with it today. The honest answer is: with a lot of reading.

  • Evaluators such as GiveWell publish very deep analyses, but only for a handful of charities.
  • Every cause uses a different unit: DALYs in health, income multipliers in poverty, learning-adjusted years in education, tonnes of CO2 in climate.
  • Charity websites mix real impact figures with outputs ("40,000 families reached"), overhead ratios, and cost per life saved, which cannot be put in the same column as cost per DALY.

Building this project's own benchmark table gives a sense of the cost. Two AI research agents worked in parallel, one per pair of causes. They took about 20 and 24 minutes (44 agent-minutes in total) to source just 9 benchmarks and 21 charities. They made 134 tool calls (searches, page fetches and PDF reads) between them, and still left 10 of the 30 numbers missing. A person doing this by hand, with source checks, would spend hours per cause.

The cost of not doing it. GiveWell's own figures show the stakes. On GiveWell's blog on 7 May 2025, a GiveWell staff member wrote that "our current estimate for the marginal cost per life saved through the Top Charities Fund is approximately $8,000", and that the actual cost "might range from roughly $5,000 to $10,000". In this tool's own table, the poverty charities range from $1.40 to $5.70 of income per dollar: a 4x spread within one cause. A donor who picks by name alone can easily give to a program that does a fraction of the good of the one next to it.

Why it has to be trustworthy. If you ask a chatbot "what is charity X's cost per DALY?", it will often give a confident number, with no source, from a model that may be years out of date. For money decisions, a wrong number is worse than no number. That is why this app's first rule is: every number has a link, a date and an exact quote, or it is marked missing.

What Dollar for Good does that those don't:

  1. One screen per cause, in the cause's own unit, with a sourced benchmark for each cause. Every row shows how many times as much good per dollar it does as that benchmark.
  2. A portfolio check for funders. Upload a CSV of grants and see which ones beat the bar, and what share of your dollars sits at or above it.
  3. An AI research agent that is not trusted. The model only finds and quotes. Code then checks four things:
    • the quote is word for word on a page the agent actually fetched;
    • the number is inside the quote;
    • the quote names the cause's unit;
    • the page is not trying to instruct an AI.
    Plain Python does all the math. A charity that can't be computed is shown as "can't compute," with the reason.

Written for donors, not analysts. Each cause card says in one line what its unit means ("A DALY is one year of healthy life"). Charities are ranked only against others measured the same way. In education, for example, a test-score-based figure is shown in its own group and not compared with the learning-adjusted benchmark. General program types, such as deworming in general, sit in their own section. Each number shows its range when the source publishes one; 5 of 20 sources do. Every "can't compute" note is one plain sentence, with the research details behind a "Show details" toggle. The tool also says openly where it stops. It explains the effective altruism idea behind it, citing the Centre for Effective Altruism's own definition, and notes that this method favors causes that are easy to measure. An AI safety tab lists organizations in their own words but does not rank them, because no comparable cost-per-outcome measure exists for that work.

Honestly, the hard expert work (GiveWell's models, the EPA's social cost of carbon) is done by others. This tool's contribution is to make that work comparable, checkable and usable on your own list of charities, and to stretch it to charities nobody has rated, without inventing numbers.

4. The agents

Design: one AI agent, surrounded by code

The app has one AI agent, the Charity Research Agent. A team of agents would have been easy to build, but I chose to make one narrow agent and put deterministic code around it. The task is to find one number and copy it exactly. Every extra agent would be another place for a number to be changed or invented. The "second opinion" is not another model; it is code that can't be talked out of its rules.

Figure 2. How work flows. Charities already in the sourced table never touch the model. For anything else, the agent fetches pages and returns a structured Extraction. Code then decides whether to believe it, and code does the math.

The agent and its tool

Tool: fetch_page(url)Reads one page as plain text. Rules:
  • Only the charity's own domain and nine evaluator domains (GiveWell, Giving What We Can, Founders Pledge, Giving Green, J-PAL, IPA, The Life You Can Save, Animal Charity Evaluators, World Bank).
  • Never private or local addresses, never links with a username or password, and redirects must stay on allowed sites.
  • Scripts, hidden elements and zero-width characters are removed, and the text is capped at 12,000 characters.
  • The result is wrapped in <untrusted_page> tags and checked by the injection detector.
Output validatorRuns the code checks before the agent's answer is accepted. If a check fails, the model gets one retry with the exact reason, for example "The rate is not a single number found in the quote". A page flagged for injection gets no retry.

Why gpt-6-luna, at low reasoning effort. It is the course model, so the teaching staff can run the app with their key. The job is reading and copying, not deep reasoning. I measured medium effort against low on the full test set (Section 5): medium cost 1.8x as much and was slower, with exactly the same accuracy.

How the benchmark table was built. The sourced table itself was made with AI help, but under the same rules. Two research agents, run in Claude Code while I built the app, gathered candidate figures. Each one had to fetch the source and copy a quote. Then the app's loader (app/data.py) re-checks every entry at startup. Any number that is not in its own quote, or has no link or no date, is downgraded to "missing." This check caught three entries on the first run. One was a benchmark the research agent had computed itself ($100 ÷ 3 = $33.33). It is now stored as the source's own "$100 → 3 LAYS," and the calculator does the division.

One real run, step by step

Here is a live run from the app (26 Oct 2026). A donor asks about One Acre Fund, website oneacrefund.org, cause Poverty.

  1. Request. The website sends name, URL and cause to POST /api/analyze. The backend checks that a key exists and that the daily token budget is not used up, then starts the agent with hard limits.
  2. Fetch 1: https://oneacrefund.org. The site is allowed (it is the charity's own domain) and returns 4,846 characters of cleaned text. The injection detector finds nothing.
  3. Fetch 2: the agent guesses /impact/financial-impact-and-sroi-results and the page does not exist. The trace marks it as a failed step, in plain words ("The page could not be opened"). If the agent tries the same URL again, the tool refuses.
  4. Fetch 3: /impact, 4,316 characters. It contains "Efficiency Social Return on Investment: $5.7 new farm profits created for every $1 of donor investment."
  5. Structured answer. The agent returns cause=poverty, figure_type=cost_and_outcome, cost_text="$1", outcome_text="$5.7", the quote, and the source URL. It does no arithmetic.
  6. Code check. The checks pass:
    • the quote is found word for word on the fetched /impact text;
    • "$1" and "$5.7" are both inside the quote, with number boundaries respected;
    • the quote contains a poverty unit (a benefit word, "profits", and a per-dollar form, "for every $1");
    • the page is not flagged.
  7. Math in code. The calculator computes 5.7 ÷ 1 = $5.70 per $1 and compares it with the poverty benchmark of $2.50 per $1 (Egger et al. 2022, cash transfer multiplier). The result is 2.3x the benchmark.
  8. Cost of this run, measured: 4 model calls, 3 page fetches, 9,050 input tokens (5,539 cached), 192 output tokens, 7.3 s, $0.0070 at the configured rates. The answer is cached, so the next lookup of One Acre Fund costs 0 tokens.

The funder view uses the same agent for unlisted rows. In the sample portfolio, Living Goods was researched by the agent. Its site states "$68 per life-year saved," which is not a DALY figure, so the row is shown as "can't compute" with that reason, instead of a converted guess.

5. Tokenomics

Read this first. Every token count here is measured from real gpt-6-luna runs. The dollar figures multiply those counts by per-token rates set in app/config.py: $1.25 per million input tokens, $0.125 per million cached input tokens, and $10 per million output tokens. These rates are placeholders that I could not confirm against the course's gpt-6-luna price list. Changing them is a one-line edit, and the app's cost display updates with them.

Per user and at scale

Even at the upper bound, the model costs about 4 cents per foundation user per month ($0.042) and about 1 cent per donor ($0.012), at the configured rates. That is far below hosting or staff time. The cost that matters at scale is keeping the sourced table current, not tokens.

What I did to keep cost down

  • No model for known charities. The sourced table and calculator answer the donor view with zero tokens.
  • Prompt caching. The long, fixed system prompt comes first, so the provider caches it. 74% of input tokens were cached in the final evaluation.
  • Answer cache. A researched charity is stored, and repeat lookups cost nothing. The sample portfolio's AI cost shows $0.0000 on a second upload.
  • Low reasoning effort and a small output: a structured answer of about 130 tokens instead of free text.
  • Hard caps per run (6 calls, 4 fetches, 60,000 tokens), per upload (10 agent rows) and per day (1,000,000 tokens).

I ran the full 18-case test set (Section 6 describes it) under each setting. All runs are real model calls, and the raw results are in eval/results/.

  • Low vs medium effort: no loss at all. Medium costs 1.8x as much for identical results, so low is the clear choice.
  • Shorter pages: 17% cheaper with identical accuracy, but I did not ship it. The synthetic test pages are short, so only the 4 real websites really tested the cut, and real pages can hide the figure past 4,000 characters. That is too small a sample to be sure the cut is safe.   • Where a cut did hurt: the first version was the cheapest per call and had the fewest checks, and it showed 2 wrong numbers. The shipped version spends slightly more per run because one in a few answers takes a retry. The input per run grew from 3,401 to 4,116 tokens, but the cost per run fell from $0.0050 to $0.0030, because the longer fixed prompt now gets cached. The safer version turned out cheaper.

6. Safety

For a tool that guides donations, the worst failure is a confident wrong number. A charity could look 10x better than it is, either because the model invented a figure or because a web page told it to. So the defenses are built around one idea: the model's answer is a claim, and code checks it against the evidence.

The tests found real bugs

The safety tests and the evaluation were not a formality. They found four real bugs, all fixed before this report:

  1. A plain substring check accepted an invented quote "averts one DALY for $9" because the page said "$92". It is now fixed with number-boundary matching.
  2. "2.5x" was parsed as 2, so the app would have shown GiveDirectly at $2.00 per $1 instead of $2.50.
  3. "CO2" broke parsing of "1.25 million tonnes of CO2."
  4. "83% of expenses delivered to recipients" was accepted as income per dollar. The poverty check now requires both an income or benefit word and a per-dollar form.

7. Impact

Accuracy: measured on 18 labelled cases

The test set has two parts:

  • 14 synthetic charity pages with known answers. 8 contain a valid figure, written in different ways ("$64 per DALY", "$2.4 million… 30,000 DALYs", "2.8 LAYS per $100", "210%", "1.25 million tonnes"). 6 should be refused: cost per life saved, no figure, a range, test scores, an offset price, and two injections.
  • 4 real charity websites fetched live: One Acre Fund, Village Enterprise, GiveDirectly and Living Goods.
VersionAccuracyWrong numbers shownFound when a figure existsRefused when none
1. First version78%264%100%
2. Parser and percent fixes78%164%100%

The headline: in the shipped version, no number the app showed was wrong (0 of 18 cases), and every trap was refused, including both prompt injections. When the app does not know, it says "can't compute" and why.

Two cases are not plain successes:

  • GiveDirectly: the agent refused a valid figure ("2.5x local economic activity"). That is a miss, not a wrong number.
  • Village Enterprise: its homepage says "$5.34 in benefits for every $1 invested," while its own blog reports a 140% benefit-cost ratio, which is what our table uses. The app's quote was real and verified, so I count it as a verified alternative. It shows a real limit: charities publish conflicting figures, and the tool proves what a page says, not whether it is right.

Time and reach

  • Listed charities: the ranking appears instantly, with the source, date and exact quote one click away.
  • An unlisted charity: the agent answers in about 6 seconds on average (7.3 s in the run in Section 4) for about $0.003. For comparison, the two AI research agents that built the table took about 20 and 24 minutes (one per pair of causes, 44 agent-minutes in total), with 67 tool calls each.
  • A whole portfolio: the 9-grant sample is checked in one upload. The funder sees which grants beat the bar and what share of dollars does (34% in the sample). This answer used to need an analyst reading annual reports.
  • Something that was hard before is now easy: putting education, health, poverty and climate grants side by side, each against its own sourced bar, in a single table.

The sample portfolio's $830,000 in grants is an illustration, not a real funder's data.

8. Limits and next steps

What doesn't work yet

  • The sourced table is small: 21 charities, 13 with usable numbers, and 7 of 9 benchmarks sourced. Many well-known charities only publish cost per life saved, which by design we do not convert to DALYs.
  • Sources are not like for like. Estimates differ by evaluator, year, method and dollar year. The AMF figure is from 2016. Climate advocacy estimates are highly uncertain expected values (their authors say to compare them only by order of magnitude), and comparing them with the social cost of carbon gives very large multiples. The app shows a caveat for each cause, but a ranking can still look more precise than it is.
  • It favors what is easy to measure. Policy change and AI safety are hard to put a number on, so they can't be ranked here. The app says so on its first screen, but a ranking still draws attention toward measurable causes.
  • The tool checks quotes, not truth. A charity's self-reported figure passes if it is really on its site, as the Village Enterprise conflict shows.
  • The agent's recall is 82% when a valid figure exists. It sometimes refuses real figures, which is the safe direction to fail.
  • The injection detector is pattern-based. A clever attacker could phrase instructions that slip past it. The quote and unit checks still apply, but a page that simply states a false figure in normal language would pass.
  • Not hosted online. It runs locally, and dollar costs depend on placeholder token rates.

With another month

  1. Grow and refresh the table: a scheduled job re-fetches every source, re-checks every quote, and flags numbers whose page changed.
  2. Show uncertainty everywhere: ranges already appear where a source publishes one (5 of 20 sources). Next, find uncertainty bands for the rest and show the ranking as intervals, not points.
  3. Read PDFs: many impact figures live in annual-report PDFs, and the fetch tool only reads web pages today.
  4. Trust tiers: mark each figure as independent evaluation, peer-reviewed study, or self-reported, and let the user filter.
  5. Pilot with a real foundation: run their actual grant list, time the before and after, and publish the result.
